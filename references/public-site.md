# Public site design and verification

Use this reference when creating or editing the public website for a plan. Choose the checks relevant to the task and the existing site.

## Page structure and copy

- Open with what would change across the area and who would benefit. Offer quick routes to the map, visual comparisons, and project materials. Help readers find a street, section, or place in a long list.
- Start each card with the place, action, and everyday benefit. State conditions that change the proposal alongside it. Distinguish existing conditions, proposals, pilots, and targets. For a transport option, name its endpoints, affected transfers, and what would be measured before selecting it.
- Use the list to find every measure, the overview to explain overall benefits, the gallery to show changes, the phasing section to explain dependencies, and the technical section to explain verification. Link to the full card when a measure appears in more than one section.
- Keep core material and phasing visible without expanding panels. Put methods, extra photographs, and detailed tables next to what they explain. Feature precedents that illustrate a specific local choice and give concise links to the other sources.
- State what was measured, where, when, and in what units. Distinguish observed results, planned measurements, and targets. Explain percentiles in everyday language. For example, “Nine out of ten trips took 14 minutes or less.”

## Keeping the map and list in sync

- Use a stable ID for each measure. Compare visible records, links, filters, and counts by ID. Each list entry should open its map card. If extra streets, squares, or pedestrian routes are counted on the map, include their measures in the complete list.
- Verify names and boundaries for neighboring proposals across the map, cards, galleries, plans, and current exports. A path to the water and a traffic pilot across the junction need their own labels and links. Date or label historical exports by edition.

## Before-and-after imagery

- Retain the original before photo, published before image, after image, camera location and direction, date or frame ID, source, and list of proposed changes. When a baseline image is reconstructed from other evidence, label it “Before · reconstruction” in the comparison and cite the source material.
- For a series audit, record the outcome for each pair. Verify the published before image against the original. For a shared viewpoint, overlay the after image on the before image at the original scale. Inspect fixed features including facades, curbs, lane markings, posts, and the background across the frame. Preserve intentional changes shown in the plan.
- When correcting a shift, use the source geometry and the list of proposed improvements as references. Check that the improvements are still visible and fit the site. Label inferred details behind occlusions where the distinction affects interpretation. Present different viewpoints separately with their own captions.
- Put extra images in the main gallery for their location and name the viewpoint or scenario. Open with the view that best explains the proposal or the order requested by the client. Verify that the map opens the intended view.

## Before handover and after publication

Open the page at narrow and wide widths. Test the paths affected by the revision, including applicable list links, map layers, sliders, and direct image links. Check source captions and footer credits. When authorized to publish, follow the project's deployment process and repeat the key checks on the public URL.
